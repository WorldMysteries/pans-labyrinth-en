About the film

Pan's Labyrinth (Spanish: El laberinto del fauno) is a dark fantasy by Mexican-Spanish director Guillermo del Toro, premiered in 2006. The story unfolds in post-civil-war Spain in 1944, where Franco's regime is still hunting rebels in the mountains. The film became an instant phenomenon: it won three Academy Awards, gathered a worldwide following and has secured its place on lists of the best films of the 21st century.

Del Toro weaves together two narrative threads that barely touch directly yet keep talking to each other: a realist war drama and a mythological fairy tale. Captain Vidal's cruelty and young Ofelia's fragile world exist in a single frame, in one palette of gold and moss — so the fairy-tale trials read not as an escape from reality but as its mirror.

The film's central idea is disobedience as a virtue. Its characters save themselves and others by refusing to obey: Ofelia does not eat the grapes, Mercedes does not betray the doctor, the doctor does not beg for his life. Del Toro has called Pan's Labyrinth his most personal film, grown largely out of childhood terrors and the memory of his grandmother's room with the door guardian.

Plot

Ofelia's three trials

Prologue · 1944

Captain Vidal's mill

Twelve-year-old Ofelia arrives with her pregnant mother Carmen at a military post commanded by her new stepfather — the brutal Captain Vidal, tasked with flushing Franco-era rebels out of the surrounding hills. In the lullaby hummed by the housekeeper Mercedes, Ofelia hears the story of a princess lost in the mortal world and of the faun waiting for her return.

First trial

The giant toad beneath the fig tree

In the ruins of the labyrinth, the Faun greets Ofelia, recognizes the spirit of Princess Moanna, and gives her the first task: find the toad rotting an ancient fig tree from within. Ofelia descends beneath the roots, feeds the toad the magic stones, and receives the golden key. Meanwhile, in the real world, Vidal brutally interrogates captured peasants, and the rebels steal medicine from his warehouse.

Second trial

The Pale Man's hall

The Faun sends Ofelia through a second door — an underground chamber where a motionless monster with eyes in his palms sits at a banquet table: the Pale Man. Frescoes on the walls show the fate of children who ate the forbidden feast. Ofelia retrieves the dagger but cannot resist and eats two grapes; the awakening eyes rise after her. Two fairies die, and Ofelia barely escapes.

Between worlds

The mandrake root and the night hunt

Ofelia's mother falls gravely ill; the Faun provides a mandrake root to be kept under her bed in a bowl of milk, calming the child in the womb. Vidal finds an outdated rebel death list, confronts the doctor Ferreiro — a spy working for Mercedes — and shoots him. Furious at Ofelia's failure, the Faun demands a third task: bring her newborn baby brother to the centre of the labyrinth at moonrise.

Finale

The princess's choice

Vidal unmasks Mercedes, but she escapes and joins the rebels. Ofelia steals her baby brother and leads him into the labyrinth; Vidal follows and corners her at a dead end. The Faun asks for a drop of innocent blood — but Ofelia refuses to harm her brother. She is shot. Vidal walks out of the labyrinth straight into Mercedes's armed ambush and dies with the reminder that his son will never even learn his name. Ofelia awakens in the fairy kingdom, where her father the king declares that she refused to spill innocent blood — the very heart of the trials.

Characters

Faces of two worlds

Ofelia

Light · Played by Ivana Baquero

A twelve-year-old girl in love with fairy tales — the daughter of a tailor killed in the war. She arrives at Vidal's post where her imagination is her only territory: a storybook found in her travel bag becomes a portal into the labyrinth.

Ofelia completes the Faun's three tasks and, in the finale, makes the film's defining choice: she refuses to spill her baby brother's blood, knowing the price of refusal. It is this disobedience — not obedience — that makes her a princess.

"She refused to spill innocent blood. That was the final and hardest of all the trials."

Captain Vidal

Darkness · Played by Sergi López

Ofelia's stepfather, a Francoist army officer convinced of his own impeccability. He winds his dead father's pocket watch every night — a pendulum of inherited obedience: evil mechanized, militarism turned into ritual.

Vidal mirrors the fairy-tale monsters: like the toad he devours the land, like the Pale Man he sits at a feast poisoned by others' deaths. He believes in neither fairy tales nor miracles — and so loses to them.

"You will die alone, and your son will never even know your name." — Mercedes

The Faun

Guardian of the labyrinth · Played by Doug Jones

An ancient creature of moss, roots and bone who has waited a thousand years for the princess's return. He is neither good nor evil — as neutral as nature itself — setting Ofelia three trials without explaining the rules or offering comfort.

Del Toro deliberately keeps the Faun ambiguous: until almost the last scene, the audience cannot be sure whether he is a saviour or simply another monster. Doug Jones plays the role — on the same shoot he spent mornings in a monster toad suit to play the Faun in pre-dawn scenes.

"You are not Moanna. You are her daughter… no. You are Moanna herself."

Mercedes

The princess's double · Played by Maribel Verdú

The housekeeper at Vidal's post and a secret liaison for the rebels; her brother leads a guerrilla band in the hills. Mercedes is the "real" Ofelia: an adult woman who passes the same trials — the lullaby, the escape, disobedience to the captain — without any magic, armed only with a knife and loyalty.

Her escape scene is textbook: a bound prisoner cuts her own ropes, wounds Vidal and vanishes into the dark, leaving behind the sentence he will hear once more at the labyrinth's edge.

The Pale Man

The devourer · Played by Doug Jones

An ancient monster whose past feeding on children made him immortal — and ruined him. He sits forever at a table heaped with food, his eyes resting in the palms of his hands laid on a platter: to see the world, he must lift them to his face.

His hall is a silent indictment of the well-fed: frescoes on the walls show children burning while monsters feast above them. The grapes Ofelia finally eats carry the film's most expensive price of failure.

Carmen

The mother · Played by Ariadna Gil

Ofelia's mother, pregnant by Vidal and trapped at his post. Fragile but not powerless: she tries to pull her daughter out of the fairy tale because she knows the cost of Vidal's cruelty, and in the pain of childbirth she nearly loses the child.

Carmen embodies the adults who accepted a compromise in order to survive — which is why the mandrake under her bed, restoring peace to the unborn child, reads as a small miracle granted not to her but to her son.

Symbols & lore

What hides in the pattern

The stone labyrinth is not a trap but a path: its spirals echo the rosette of the underground kingdom, and Ofelia's descent into it mirrors her journey into and out of the womb. The labyrinth is a place of choice, where the fairy tale demands not cunning but conscience.

Composer Javier Navarrete built the whole score around a simple three-note lullaby (D, A and B♭). The melody sounds in the king's urn, at Vidal's post and in the princess's final awakening, binding the war story and the fairy tale into one rhythm.

Every night Vidal winds his father's pocket watch. The inheritance is not memory but repetition: he dies by the same mechanism his father did, an anonymous death. The watch is the film's direct answer to the question of where obedience to orders ends.

The mandrake root, which screams when torn from the earth, is in folklore a symbol of dangerous life outside the law. Under Carmen's bed it hums the lullaby — until Vidal notices it: the fairy-tale protection collapses exactly where reality refuses to see it.

Every salvation in the film is an act of defiance: Ofelia refuses the grapes, Mercedes refuses to give in, the doctor Ferreiro refuses to beg for his life. Del Toro builds his morality on breaking rules: an order, ordered greed, ordered fear — those are the true monsters, not people.
